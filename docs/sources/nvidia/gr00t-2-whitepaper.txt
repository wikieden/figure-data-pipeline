1. Introduction
Building general-purpose robots requires models that can acquire diverse physical skills, generalize across
tasks and embodiments, and adapt efficiently to new environments and objectives. Recent advances in
vision-language-action (VLA) models have demonstrated the potential of large-scale pretraining for robotic
manipulation. However, scaling robot intelligence involves challenges beyond learning from increasingly
large demonstration datasets. A general-purpose robot foundation model must learn from heterogeneous
sources of physical experience, acquire new capabilities with limited task-specific supervision, improve through
interaction, and support reliable, responsive execution in complex robotic systems. Furthermore, as robots
become increasingly autonomous, their learned physical capabilities must be accessible to higher-level reasoning
and planning systems. Meeting these requirements calls for models that capture not only how robots act, but
also how their actions change the physical world.
We introduce GR00T 2, a new generation of foundation world-action models (WAMs). GR00T 2 jointly predicts
future visual observations and embodied actions, learning how physical interactions unfold alongside the
actions that produce them. Through large-scale pretraining on diverse human and robot experience, GR00T 2
develops general-purpose manipulation capabilities across both parallel-gripper robots and dexterous hands.
We demonstrate four major advances. First, GR00T 2 delivers strong out-of-the-box performance across
diverse manipulation tasks and embodiments. Second, its pretrained capabilities enable rapid adaptation
to challenging new tasks from remarkably few demonstrations, while reinforcement learning (RL) further
improves manipulation reliability through real-world interaction. Third, GR00T 2 enables x-adaptation (cross
adaptation), allowing physical knowledge acquired from one embodiment to be transferred to another through
joint prediction of future observations and actions. Finally, GR00T 2 supports agentic control through two
complementary capabilities: skill composition and recovery with code-as-policy (CaP), enabling higher-level
agents to orchestrate manipulation and recover from execution failures, and steerable world-action generation
through a WAM API, allowing agents to guide and compose robot behaviors using visual targets.
Emergence of X-Adaptability. A central innovation of GR00T 2 is its cross-adaptation (x-adapt) capability,
which allows the model to generalize to task-scene-embodiment combinations unseen during training. Consider
a manipulation task observed only in human demonstrations and a robot embodiment represented by data
from an entirely different set of tasks. Through joint world-action pretraining, GR00T 2 learns to imagine
how the task would unfold when performed by the target robot and to generate actions corresponding to that
imagined interaction. This creates a mechanism for transferring physical knowledge across heterogeneous
sources of experience, including UMI demonstrations, dexterous UMI data, human hand interactions, and robot
embodiments with substantially different kinematics. We explore this capability through cross-embodiment
transfer experiments and a zero-teleoperation dexterous learning system, demonstrating the potential to
acquire robot skills directly from human experience without collecting task-specific robot demonstrations. More
broadly, cross-embodiment imagination points toward a new scaling property of robot foundation models:
physical behaviors learned during pretraining can become executable capabilities across embodiments.
Fast Adaptation and Agentic Control. GR00T 2 advances the capabilities of pretrained robot policies across
generalization, dexterity, and adaptation. A diverse pretraining mixture equips the model with a broad repertoire
of physical behaviors, enabling strong out-of-the-box performance and rapid acquisition of challenging new
skills from few demonstrations. We further extend these capabilities through RL to improve manipulation
reliability and efficient inference for responsive, closed-loop control. Building on these learned skills, we
integrate GR00T 2 with higher-level reasoning agents that use CaP to compose manipulation skills, monitor
execution, and recover from unexpected failures, bringing GR00T 2 closer to deployment-level reliability
on challenging long-horizon manipulation tasks. Beyond skill composition and recovery, GR00T 2’s joint
world-action representation provides a natural interface for steering robot behavior through desired visual
outcomes and intermediate future states. We develop a WAM API that exposes this capability to higher-level
agents, allowing them to guide and compose robot behaviors through visual targets. Together, these capabilities

Realizing these advances requires innovations throughout the robot learning stack. We first describe the
GR00T 2 architecture and pretraining framework, including Human-1M, our large-scale corpus of embodied
human experience (Sec. 4.2.1), and the data processing and training infrastructure that support learning from
heterogeneous sources. We then investigate cross-embodiment transfer and introduce a dexterous human-to-
robot learning system that combines motion retargeting, visual alignment, and world-action modeling. Beyond
pretraining, we develop reinforcement learning methods for improving manipulation through interaction and a
steerable WAM API that enables agentic planning and guided execution.
We evaluate GR00T 2 across a broad suite of simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, spanning general-
purpose policy performance, few-shot adaptation, cross-embodiment transfer, dexterous manipulation, and
whole-body humanoid control. Our results demonstrate the versatility of world-action pretraining as a foun-
dation for acquiring and transferring physical skills, and highlight the complementary roles of large-scale
pretraining, interactive post-training, and system-level integration. Together, GR00T 2 represents a step toward
general-purpose robot foundation models whose physical knowledge can be transferred across embodiments,
efficiently adapted to new tasks, and directly accessed and directed by intelligent agents.


2. GR00T 2 Overview
GR00T 2 is a world-action model (WAM): a single model that, given the robot’s visual and proprioceptive
input and a user instruction, predicts both how the scene will unfold and the robot’s actions. One pretrained
checkpoint covers parallel-gripper arms, dexterous hands, and a humanoid, and learns diverse source data
sources including human egocentric videos, handheld UMI gripper data, and robot teleopration data. (Sec. 4.2).
Inputs and outputs. At every control step, GR00T 2 receives (i) a 0.8s history of 224 × 320 images from up to
four cameras at 5 fps, typically a head camera and one camera on each wrist, (ii) the robot’s proprioceptive
state, and (iii) a natural-language instruction. It returns a chunk of future actions together with the predicted
future frames of every camera. The robot executes the chunk through its own controller while the next chunk
is computed (Sec. K.5).
Architecture. GR00T 2 is built on Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B, a video diffusion transformer pretrained for world
simulation (NVIDIA et al., 2025a). Video, state, and action inputs become tokens in one sequence that a single
2B-parameter transformer processes (Fig. 1):
   • Video. A frozen Wan 2.1 video VAE (Wan Team et al., 2025) compresses each camera stream 4× in time
     and 8× in each spatial dimension. The latents are split into patches, giving 280 tokens per latent frame
     and camera at 224 × 320. A learned view embedding tells the model which camera each token comes
     from. Past frames enter as context, and future frames are generated.
   • Language. A frozen Cosmos-Reason1 vision-language model (NVIDIA et al., 2025b) encodes the instruction,
     and the transformer reads it through cross-attention.
   • State and actions. Small embodiment-specific MLPs map the robot state and the noisy action chunk into
     the transformer’s width, and an embodiment-specific decoder maps the output tokens back to actions.
Inside the transformer, video tokens attend to one another across time and cameras, and action tokens attend
to all video tokens, including the future frames being generated. Actions can therefore follow the future the
model imagines. GR00T 2 is trained with flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) on future video latents and
actions together, with one loss for each. At inference, a few denoising steps suffice: one or two for video and
two to four for actions in our deployments (Sec. I.1).

(Sec. 3.1 and Fig. 23).
Specifically, Turbo replaces the video VAE with the Wan 2.2 VAE (Wan Team et al., 2025), increasing spatial
compression from 8× to 16× per dimension and producing a 4× smaller spatial latent grid. The latent input
and output projections are adapted to the new VAE, while the action horizon remains unchanged.
Using GR00T 2 on a new robot. To bring up a robot, a practitioner maps its cameras to the head and wrist
views, expresses its actions in a supported action space (or adds a new one), and post-trains GR00T 2 on
demonstrations of the target tasks. Sec. 3 shows how few demonstrations this takes, and how to replace
teleoperation with human or UMI demonstrations altogether.


3. GR00T 2 Capabilities
We now present the capabilities of GR00T 2. Sec. 3.1 shows zero-shot deployment and few-shot adaptation on
new tasks and post-training on long-horizon tasks that demand precision and dexterity, and Sec. 3.2 studies
cross-embodiment transfer, in which GR00T 2 learns a skill on a robot without teleoperated demonstrations of
that skill. Sec. 3.3 shows that reinforcement learning improves GR00T 2 beyond its demonstrations, Sec. 3.4
pairs GR00T 2 with a frontier agent for long-horizon tasks, and Sec. 3.5 extends GR00T 2 to whole-body
humanoid loco-manipulation. We report the main results here and defer the full evaluations to the appendix.

3.1. Fast Adaptation: Zero-Shot Generalization and Few-Shot Mastery
Zero-shot deployment on new tasks. Pretraining on teleoperation data from many robots and on Human-1M
human video (Sec. 4.2) gives GR00T 2 an emergent ability to perform tasks it has never seen on the target
robot. We test this on Dexter (Tianji robot + Sharpa hands), a bimanual robot with two 22-DoF dexterous
hands, with 50 tasks that are absent from Dexter’s own data and appear only in YAM gripper data, human
video, or both. Deployed out of the box and conditioned only on the language instruction, GR00T 2 succeeds
in 40% of trials and completes 40 of the 50 tasks at least once (Fig. 2). Most failures come from a single step

3.2. X-Adaptability: Cross-Embodiment Skill Transfer
GR00T 2 can learn a new task on a robot without any task-specific teleoperated demonstration. Fig. 4
summarizes four ways to teach it such a task, from demonstrations recorded with a motion-capture glove, the
DexUMI exoskeleton, or the handheld Gripper UMI, and from a single human video expanded in simulation.

                   GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



For the glove and Gripper UMI demonstrations, which come from a different embodiment than the robot, one
recipe bridges the gap. Demonstrations recorded without the robot specify the task, task-agnostic play data
from the target robot specify the embodiment, and cross-embodiment pretraining connects the two. Without
this pretraining, the same post-training data yield substantially lower performance (Sec. B.1.5). For the glove,
DexUMI, and Gripper UMI demonstrations, the baselines are post-trained on the same data as GR00T 2 (Fig. 27
and Tabs. 4 and 5). When teleoperated demonstrations are available, they complement rather than replace the
human data (App. B).
Transfer to dexterous hands. Two devices record dexterous demonstrations without the robot: a motion-
capture glove worn on the bare hand, and the DexUMI exoskeleton, which replicates the robot hand’s joints and
contact surfaces and records fingertip forces in the hand’s tactile format. With the glove, human demonstrations
are recorded with head and wrist cameras, retargeted offline to the robot hand, and co-trained with play
data of the target robot (Sec. B.1.3). On a bimanual robot with Sharpa hands, this recipe teaches precise
chemistry-lab tasks and a fast conveyor toss that cannot be demonstrated through teleoperation. On one task,
GR00T 2 trained without any teleoperated demonstration outperforms a baseline without cross-embodiment
pretraining that receives ten (Fig. 27). DexUMI extends robot-free collection to tasks that depend on force and
compliance: from its force recordings, GR00T 2 learns to predict the force to apply along with the motion, and
the hand’s stiffness adapts to that force. Trained on DexUMI demonstrations alone, GR00T 2 slices a banana
and unscrews a jar in a long-horizon breakfast task and lights a lamp with a struck match, with 60 to 80%
success per task, whereas GR00T 1.7 and 𝜋0.5 complete none (Fig. 15 and Tab. 5). DexUMI demonstrations
also transfer to a hand that the exoskeleton does not replicate. After retargeting to the 22-DoF Sharpa hand,
GR00T 2 succeeds in 60% of trials on microscope slide dabbing and 65% on conveyor package selection and
tossing, and the same two baselines succeed in none (Tab. 6).
Transfer to parallel-gripper robots. Demonstrations are recorded with the handheld Gripper UMI, in robot
format through robot-style wrist cameras and a width encoder, and deployed on YAM, a robot that shares
neither its cameras, nor its gripper, nor its workspace. GR00T 2 co-trains them with task-agnostic YAM play
data that supply the embodiment and aligns them to YAM’s camera geometry and action space (Sec. 4.4.2).
With 41 to 75 demonstrations per task and no teleoperation, GR00T 2 completes a long-horizon toy assembly
and a board disassembly in 60% and 50% of trials and sorts 86% of the items off a moving conveyor, whereas
the baselines complete neither long-horizon task (Tab. 4). As few as 10 to 20 demonstrations yield end-to-end
success. Collection is thus decoupled from the robot, and human-speed motion makes dynamic skills learnable.
Transfer from a single human video through simulation. From one human video, we reconstruct the objects
and the hand-object motion, train an RL expert in simulation, and fine-tune GR00T 2 on visually randomized
rollouts of that expert. The resulting policy transfers zero-shot to the real robot and drives a nail in 9 of 10
trials, whereas GR00T 1.7 and 𝜋0.5 complete none of the real-world tasks (Sec. B.5).

3.4. Agentic GR00T 2

Agentic Steering. With the agent acting as System 2 and GR00T 2 as System 1, a central design question is
what the interface between them should be, that is, what the agent sends to GR00T 2 for each step; we refer to
this interface as the WAM API. Language is the natural choice, but grounding it in the scene is hard: GR00T 2
must itself find the objects and locations that an instruction refers to. We therefore explore guiding GR00T 2
with image-space grasp and release points in addition to language: the points specify directly which object
to manipulate, where to place it, and which arm acts. We mid-train GR00T 2 to condition on these points,
rendered as arm-colored markers on its camera image alongside the language instruction. Point supervision
is generated automatically from the gripper and hand states in existing demonstrations, requiring neither
additional manual annotation nor new robot demonstrations. Once mid-trained, GR00T 2 can be steered by
agent-generated points without task-specific policy fine-tuning. During execution, GR00T 2 generates the
robot’s actions, while the agent monitors progress and revises its guidance to advance, retry, or finish. In these
experiments, the agent is used zero-shot, with one conversation per episode (Sec. G.2).

3.5. Humanoid Loco-Manipulation
Bipedal humanoid loco-manipulation poses additional challenges to robotic foundation models: the robot
must stay balanced through every motion, which requires high-frequency whole-body control at 50 Hz. We
bring GR00T 2 to this setting by pairing GR00T 2 Turbo with SONIC (Luo et al., 2026), a learned whole-body
controller (Fig. 9). Turbo uses camera observations and proprioception to choose what motion to take next, and
SONIC turns that desired motion into joint commands for execution. Both models run onboard the humanoid
using a multi-layer stack (Sec. H.1).
Whole-body Loco-manipulation. Using SONIC’s whole-body motion latent interface as its action space,
GR00T 2 Turbo coordinates the humanoid’s entire body for locomotion and manipulation. We evaluate the
resulting policies on four real-world long horizon whole-body tasks. In popcorn serving, the robot holds a cup
while scooping and pouring with the other hand. In table clean-up, it repeatedly grasps diverse objects, walks
to the trash bins, and places them inside. In ground pick-up, it bends down to pick up diverse objects. In
whole-body cleaning, it picks up a cup from the floor, wipes a spill with a towel under its foot, then kneels to
retrieve and discard the towel. On popcorn serving, Turbo completes all 48 trials, compared with 83.3% for
GR00T 1.7 using the same demonstrations and controller. Each trial requires two filled cups. Detailed results
for all four real-world tasks are reported in Sec. H.2 and Tab. 25.
Sim-and-real for Loco-manipulation. Whole-body teleoperation data is costly to collect, so we design a
motion-centric real-to-sim pipeline that turns each recorded motion into demonstrations across varied scenes.
We replay the motion through SONIC in simulation, keeping it fixed while fitting table and object placements
so that the robot completes the pick, then render successful rollouts in diverse rooms. These demonstrations
expand visual diversity while preserving the real robot’s controller and action representation (Sec. H.3). The
WAM makes better use of this simulated data than the VLA baseline on out-of-distribution table pick-and-place:
starting from the same 6.7% success with real data alone, co-training raises GR00T 2 Turbo to 63.3%, compared
with 40.0% for GR00T 1.7, over 30 trials each (Tab. 26). Reusing real motions in simulation therefore provides a
practical way to improve the WAM’s generalization without collecting additional teleoperated demonstrations.

4.1. Training: Pre-Training and RL Post-Training
Pre-training. We initialize GR00T 2 from the pretrained Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B video diffusion transformer.
To turn this video model into a general-purpose robot policy, we need it both to understand how physical
interactions unfold and to connect those dynamics to actions across embodiments. We therefore train on a
diverse mixture of human and robot experience in three stages: we first adapt the video model to embodied
interactions, then learn video and actions jointly, and finally place greater emphasis on robot data. This
progression lets us draw on the breadth of human experience while focusing the resulting model on robot
execution.
Stage 1: Single-view video pre-training. We first adapt the pretrained video model to embodied visual
experience from egocentric human video, supplemented by robot data. Human demonstrations provide diverse
examples of hands interacting with objects across tasks and environments, while robot observations introduce
the visual conditions of robot manipulation. Each training example uses a single camera view, and the model is
trained to predict future video from the observation history. The goal is to establish a visual dynamics prior for
embodied interaction before training the model to generate actions.
Stage 2: Multi-view video-action training. We next connect the learned visual dynamics to actions by
extending the model with embodiment-specific state and action modules. We jointly train video and action
prediction on human and robot data, using multi-view observations where available to capture interactions
from complementary viewpoints. Robot trajectories provide embodiment-specific actions, while human demon-
strations provide tracked hand-pose trajectories as action supervision. Video and action are trained with
flow-matching objectives, and the action loss is applied when valid action supervision is available. The mixture
increases the contribution of YAM and dexterous-hand sources while retaining substantial egocentric human
data. Joint training is intended to bring experience from these different embodiments into a shared model that
can predict both the evolution of a scene and actions for the target robot.
Stage 3: Robot-upweighted video-action training. A broad mixture of human and robot data provides
diverse physical experience, but the final policy must generate actions in the robot’s own action space. We
therefore continue joint video-action training with a stronger emphasis on robot demonstrations, particularly
YAM, while retaining dexterous-hand, egocentric human, and other data sources. This reweighting focuses
training on the observations and control required for robot execution while continuing to draw on the broader
experience acquired in the earlier stages.
The resulting model serves as the starting point for the task-specific adaptation and RL post-training described
below. Details of the data sources are provided in Sec. 4.2.
RL post-training. To explore, we replace GR00T 2’s deterministic action denoising with Coefficients-Preserving
Sampling (CPS) (Wang and Yu, 2025) during rollouts, injecting noise only into the action denoising process
while video denoising stays deterministic. Policy updates use a group-relative objective in the style of GRPO
whose baseline also remembers each context’s past returns, so that groups in which every attempt succeeds, or
every attempt fails, still carry a learning signal. The basis of this recipe was found by AutoRL, a research agent
that iterated on the algorithm in simulation for about 35 days (Sec. F.2). Two complementary approaches
are GRPO-induced self-imitation, which fine-tunes GR00T 2 on successful GRPO rollouts, and residual RL,
which learns a small corrective policy on top of a frozen GR00T 2 (Sec. F.1.2). For RL without the real robot,
we adapt the DreamDojo world model to the target tasks from rollouts of the initial policy and use a learned

4.2. Data
GR00T 2 pretrains on human demonstrations, UMI demonstrations, and robot teleoperation, which play
complementary roles. Egocentric human video from Human-1M provides broad coverage of physical interactions
and environments, and robot performance grows with the amount of it (Sec. 4.2.1). UMI provides scalable,
task-focused demonstrations with robot-compatible action representations (Sec. 4.2.2), and teleoperation
grounds the model in embodiment-specific behaviors and executable actions (Sec. 4.2.3). Finally, Flora, our
data engine, labels the subtask annotation of human and robot episodes at this scale (Sec. 4.2.4). App. C details
the collection, processing, and curation of each source, and Sec. 4.7.3 describes the pipeline that converts raw
data into training-ready format.

4.2.1. Human Data
For a world action model, egocentric human experience captures the motion and visual diversity needed to
achieve strong coverage across objects, affordances, tasks, scenes and environments. GR00T 2 leverages 402,730
hours across approximately 13.75 million episodes, spanning over 51,500 unique collectors and 123,000 scenes
of high quality egocentric data filtered from Human-1M. The dataset is a 1 million hour egocentric human
experience dataset of proficient human labor sensorized with egocentric cameras, dense language annotations,
SLAM and 3D hand pose keypoints curated by NVIDIA, spanning demonstrations in both natural “in-the-wild”
and controlled studio environments. Additional details on filters and dataset composition in Sec. C.1.
In-lab human data. To enable direct transfer to the target robot embodiment, GR00T 2 contains 3,000 hours
of robot-aligned studio data, collected under a stricter protocol. The visual gap is mitigated by matching the
egocentric and wrist camera viewpoints, while the motion gap is mitigated by matching demonstration speed
with the robot. This data is demonstration-style where each task is demonstrated hundreds of times with a
repeatable strategy (Sec. C.1.3).
Human Data Scaling. Under the same GR00T 2 architecture, training recipe and post-training teleoperation
data, we ablate four Human-1M data pre-training scales (0%, 1%, 10%, or 100%). We control training length
in epochs. The 1%, 10%, and 100% models start from a shared human-video pretraining checkpoint and
are each pretrained for about 0.5 epoch of their share, while the 0% model skips human-video pretraining

                   GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



entirely. The step from 0% to 1% therefore also includes this shared stage, and the 1%, 10%, and 100% models
differ only in their share of human data and the matching training length. Here one epoch means the model
passes once over every step of every 30 Hz trajectory. Every model is then trained on the same target Dexter
teleoperation data, co-trained with its human share, for the same 3 epochs of that robot data. We measure real
robot and video rollout success rate (%) and task progress (%) on 72 Dexter tasks, 22 out-of-distribution tasks
with new kinds of instructions and 50 tasks absent from Dexter’s training data. Across all four settings, we
see positive scaling in performance as human data is scaled (Fig. 10). Real robot success rates increase from
18% to 41% from 0% human data pre-training to 100%. Video rollout success rates similarly increase from
30% to 51%. Together, these results show that what GR00T 2 learns from human video transfers to the robot.
With the robot data held fixed, pretraining on all of the multi-view human video more than doubles real-robot
success on new tasks, and none of the metrics levels off at 100%, which suggests that more human video will
keep improving robot performance (Sec. C.1.5).

4.2.2. UMI Data
UMI-10K, recorded with the handheld UMI device (App. E), contributes 942k quality-controlled episodes and
10,184 hours of bimanual parallel-gripper demonstrations across 2,897 tasks. The dataset is extremely diverse
with no single task contributing more than 1.2% of hours. The instructions span 356 object categories and 49
dextrous skills. The corpus covers 2,798 distinct skill-object combinations, including dynamic, articulated, and
deformable manipulation that teleoperation rarely captures. Many tasks contain counterfactual pairs, which is
aimed to improve policy instruction following (Sec. C.2).

4.2.3. Teleoperation Data
Cross-embodiment teleoperated demonstrations provide robot action supervision for GR00T 2. The pre-training
mixture contains a total of 23.5k hours and 1.16M episodes of teleoperation data including:
   • Bimanual YAM arms with parallel grippers (18.7k hours, including ABC-130k (XDOF, 2026)),
   • Bimanual arms with Sharpa hands (2.9k hours),
   • Bimanual arms with Wuji hands (1.3k hours),
   • The Unitree G1 humanoid (275 hours),
   • The single-arm Franka Panda from DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024).
Additional details are provided in Sec. C.3.

4.2.4. Data Annotation with Flora
GR00T 2 pretraining uses dense timestamped language annotations for language conditioning, filtering and
rebalancing. GR00T 2 RL post-training additionally requires process rewards, while policy evaluation requires
outcome labelling. At the scale of Human-1M and our robot corpora, human labeling is too slow and frontier API
models are too expensive. To provide these dense labels, we leverage Flora (Leng et al., 2026), the data engine
that annotates training and evaluation data for GR00T 2. Flora consists of three models that identify action
boundaries, annotate each action with a self-contained imperative instruction, and generate outcome, dense,
and rubric-based rewards. On WGO-Bench (Macrodata Labs, 2026), Flora achieves a temporal segmentation
F1 of 50.3, compared to 46.0 for the next-best model, GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026). Additional details are
provided in Sec. C.4.

4.3. Real-World Benchmarking: Mapping the Hill We Climb
Every GR00T 2 candidate earned its place in blinded head-to-head matches on real robots, accumulated

operator, the hardware, and the day, so a score from one session cannot be compared with a score from another.

A blinded A/B trial runs two to four checkpoints in randomized order on the same scene, by the same operator,
within minutes, so the drift is shared and cancels in the comparison. GearArena, our real-robot leaderboard,
pools every such trial. By the snapshot used here it held 195 A/B campaigns with 16,044 graded robot episodes
and 11,767 pairwise comparisons, collected on 10 YAM stations by more than 20 operators between July and
November 2026, rating 71 checkpoints.
Tasks from first principles. We design evaluation tasks by asking what the robot should be able to do, then
choosing tasks that expose those capabilities, and finally gauging them on real checkpoints: a task every
checkpoint completes is archived, and one no checkpoint completes is shelved until models catch up, since
neither separates anything. Two suites resulted: a first suite of general and dexterous manipulation tasks and
the current suite of 38 language-following and motion-primitive tasks, each run with partial-credit rubrics.
From matches to a map. Every pair of checkpoints in a trial forms one comparison, decided by the operator’s
explicit ranking (equal grades count as ties). A Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952), fit jointly to all
comparisons, gives each checkpoint an Elo rating. Within each head-to-head, every task carries equal weight,
and confidence intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples tasks rather than trials. Every new checkpoint
is first matched against the A/B reference, the best-rated checkpoint on the board, which keeps all ratings on
one connected scale (App. D).

to 325), an expected 80% head-to-head win rate, and GR00T 1.7 by 95 Elo (CI 50 to 148), both in all 500
bootstrap resamples (Tab. 13). On our YAM V2 task suite, which focuses on foundational skills such as language
following and motion primitives (Tab. 2), GR00T 2 Turbo is statistically indistinguishable from GR00T 2 in skill
while running each inference about three times faster (52 vs. 171 ms per served call on a GeForce RTX 5090
workstation, Fig. 23). Over four months, the best checkpoint on the current suite climbed 206 Elo, from a June

4.4.2. Gripper UMI
Gripper UMI is a handheld parallel gripper in the lineage of UMI (Chi et al., 2024). We use our open-source
GEAR UMI (NVIDIA GEAR Team, 2026): its robot-style wrist cameras and width encoder record human
demonstrations in robot coordinates without a robot (Sec. E.1), and it also yields the UMI-10K pretraining
corpus (Sec. 4.2.2). A robot end-effector that reproduces the handheld’s cameras and fingers removes the
sensing and gripper gaps by construction (Sec. E.2). For an off-the-shelf robot such as YAM, which shares
none of them, three findings make the transfer work. (1) Co-training with task-agnostic YAM data closes the
appearance and scene gap: GR00T 2 then predicts YAM’s arms performing the handheld-demonstrated task
(Fig. 14). (2) The model absorbs appearance but not geometry, so the fisheye wrist view is re-rendered to
imitate YAM’s wrist camera, and a rough match of field of view and viewing direction suffices. (3) Handheld
actions are re-expressed in YAM’s action space and decoded by its own action head. With cross-embodiment
pretraining, 41 to 75 handheld episodes per task reach high success rates, and 10 to 20 already yield end-to-end
success. The rarest subskill, not the task length, sets this number (Sec. B.2 and E.3).

4.4.4. Human-to-Sim-to-Real
Contact-rich skills such as in-hand reorientation and forceful tool use remain difficult for WAMs to learn, and
recovery behaviors are difficult to demonstrate. Reinforcement learning in simulation supplies them from a
single human video (Fig. 16). The NVIDIA Video to Data pipeline (NVIDIA, 2026b) reconstructs the object
assets and the hand and object motion. A state-based RL expert, trained with CHORD (Zhu et al., 2026) under
identified robot dynamics and randomized physics, learns to track the object motion and complete the task.
Its rollouts become image-based training data, perturbed by random action bursts and teleports so that they
contain corrections, and rendered in Isaac Lab with randomized appearance, lighting, and camera calibration.
GR00T 2 is fine-tuned on 1.5k such episodes per task with training-time real-time chunking (Sec. 4.6.2) and
deployed on the real robot without any real-world robot data (Sec. B.5).

4.5.1. Real-to-Sim Task Construction
We recreate in simulation the real-world tasks used to develop GR00T 2, from general manipulation tasks
evaluated zero-shot to multi-stage assembly, with rigid and articulated objects, deformable materials, and
particles (Fig. 17). The aim is to reproduce both the visual setup and the physical interactions that determine
whether a task succeeds. Agent-assisted workflows build the environments: agents use inputs such as real
robot videos, 3D scans, and photographs, along with tools from SimFoundry (Ranawaka et al., 2026), to
handle scene and asset reconstruction, including geometry, appearance, and collision modeling. Human experts
review the resulting content and provide feedback to guide refinement. The simulation environments run in
Isaac Lab Arena (NVIDIA Isaac Lab-Arena Contributors, 2025) on Newton (The Newton Contributors, 2025)
with the MuJoCo Warp solver (Google DeepMind and NVIDIA, 2025). Environments are validated through
several means including grasp and drop tests (for assets), replaying real robot demonstrations, and for assembly
environments, testing assembly without a robot (Sec. J.1). For the humanoid, a separate motion-centric pipeline
turns teleoperated whole-body motions into simulated demonstrations (Sec. 3.5).

4.5.2. Simulation as a Proxy for Real-World Evaluation
Policies trained on real data are evaluated in these environments directly, without fine-tuning or co-training on
simulation data. On 25 YAM tasks, 14 GR00T 2 checkpoints evaluated in both settings, with 5 blinded A/B

trials on the real robot and 10 simulated trials per task, give Pearson correlations of 0.93 for task progress,
0.92 for success rate, and 0.93 for Bradley-Terry Elo ratings. These values are 95 to 99% of the correlation that

4.5.3. Neural Simulation
Physics simulation requires reconstructing each task, whereas a learned world model can be built from recorded
robot interaction. We turn DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026), a world model initialized from pretrained video
priors, into an environment in which GR00T 2 collects RL experience (Sec. 4.1). DreamDojo is first mid-trained
on multi-view robot episodes, 8,310 from YAM (about 227 hours, including failed attempts and recoveries)
and 57,773 from DROID (about 273 hours), with the camera views tiled into one video canvas so that they
are predicted jointly. It is then adapted to the target tasks from a few hundred successful and failed rollouts
of the initial policy per task. In closed loop, GR00T 2 predicts an action chunk, the world model predicts the
multi-view frames that result from executing it, and these frames become GR00T 2’s next observation. The
world model carries its history in latent space, and the last commanded action stands in for the robot state.
Because each episode chains many short predictions, the world model conditions on its own imperfect outputs.
For the model used in RoboLab, perturbing the conditioning latents with noise during training exposes it to
such histories. Rewards come from compact video classifiers that detect task success, trained offline on labels
that a vision-language model proposes and humans review. A sparse success reward is used because dense
progress scores are expensive to annotate and can be exploited by the policy. RL in this environment raises the
success of GR00T 2 from 43% to 72% on average over four real-world tasks (peg insertion, rack-hole insertion,
and bun placement on two cups; Fig. 52) and from 51% to 71% on ten RoboLab tasks, both measured outside
the learned environment (Sec. F.5).

4.5.4. Simulation Benchmarks
We evaluate GR00T 2 on five public benchmarks with their official task sets, episode budgets, and success
criteria, using for each a single checkpoint post-trained from the same pretrained model (Sec. J.3). Baseline
results are taken from the official leaderboards as of November 6, 2026, except GR00T 1.7, which we retrain
and evaluate ourselves. Among VLA and WAM policies on the public leaderboards, GR00T 2 achieves the
highest success rate on RoboCasa365 (Nasiriany et al., 2026), a single-arm mobile manipulation benchmark
(67.2%, 2.8 points above the best prior entry), and on RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026a), a bimanual benchmark
(35.5%). On RoboLab (Yang et al., 2026) and MolmoSpaces (Kim et al., 2026), it places third (47.4%) and
second (45.1%) after post-training only on real DROID data, so both evaluations are zero-shot real-to-sim
transfer. On the G1 Factory humanoid benchmark (NVIDIA, 2026c), it reaches 84.7% success against 69.4%
for GR00T 1.7. With RL post-training and agentic execution, GR00T 2 ranks first on the RoboCasa365 and
RoboDojo leaderboards, the latter evaluated on one of its three official layout banks (Sec. 3.4 and Tab. 19).

4.6.1. Policy Serving and Accelerated Inference
GR00T 2 is served through a client-server interface: a robot client sends the camera history, robot state, and
instruction, and the server returns an action chunk, which the client buffers and executes at the controller’s
rate while it requests the next one (Sec. K.5). The server first removes repeated computation. It reuses the
text features while the instruction is unchanged, precomputes the timestep conditioning, and lets action-only
denoising steps reuse the attention keys and values of the latest video step. It then compiles the video encoder
and the transformer into TensorRT engines replayed with CUDA graphs, for supported checkpoints applies
FP8 to selected projection and feed-forward layers after calibration on robot observations, and reduces the
number of denoising steps where task success and smoothness allow. On the serving path, streaming and
prefetching move video encoding ahead of the prediction request. For GR00T 2 Turbo on the G1, TensorRT
compilation gives the largest single reduction on both GPUs (Fig. 19). Together, these optimizations reduce
median prediction latency by 2.9× to 7.2× relative to eager BF16 execution of the same checkpoint (Tab. 27).

prefetched. For the Unitree G1, GR00T 2 Turbo predicts in 38 ms on a GeForce RTX 5090 and in 124 ms
on the robot’s onboard Jetson Thor, where it plans 1.6 s chunks of whole-body motion tokens about once
per second for the SONIC controller to execute at 50 Hz (Fig. 9). Numerical comparisons with eager BF16
execution help identify changes in predicted actions, but prediction error alone does not establish task success
in closed-loop control. We make deployment decisions to screen inference changes and make deployment
decisions through evaluation on the physical robot, considering task success, completion time, and motion
quality together (Sec. I.1).

4.6.3. Accelerating Task Throughput Beyond Demonstrations
Continuous execution and low latency do not by themselves boost task completion throughput, which remains
bound to the pace of the demonstrations. We therefore retime GR00T 2’s action chunks by task phase. A task
is decomposed into semantically meaningful phases, such as reaching, grasping, transporting, and screwing,
which tolerate different degrees of acceleration. An agent searches for a per-phase speed schedule by evaluating
accelerated rollouts and refining the schedule after observed failures. At deployment, a lightweight subtask
predictor, trained on phase annotations generated by an agent, predicts from the current observation the phases
spanned by each action chunk, and the chunk is retimed according to the schedule (Fig. 20). Because retiming
changes the timing between successive chunks, temporal chunk alignment and cross-chunk jerk smoothing
keep the execution continuous (Sec. I.3).

4.7. Infrastructure
Training GR00T 2 runs a joint video-action model over multi-camera video from human, UMI, and robot
sources. Its infrastructure must provide the memory and compute for this model at scale, deliver decoded video
windows to every GPU without stalling synchronous training, and turn raw recordings into training-ready data
at the scale of the pretraining corpus. We describe the pretraining compute and distributed training runtime
(Sec. 4.7.1), the training data loader (Sec. 4.7.2), and the multimodal data processing pipeline (Sec. 4.7.3).
Policy serving is described with deployment (Sec. 4.6.1), and the online RL infrastructure with RL post-training
(Sec. 4.1).

4.7.1. Pretraining Compute and Distributed Training
GR00T 2 is pretrained on NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 systems, chosen for GPU memory capacity, Tensor Core
throughput, and acceleration of collective communication. Each Blackwell Ultra GPU provides up to 288 GB of
HBM3e with 8 TB/s of bandwidth and about 2.5 PFLOPS of dense BF16/FP16 Tensor Core compute. A rack
connects 72 GPUs into one NVLink domain with 1.8 TB/s of bidirectional bandwidth per GPU, in which NVLink
SHARP offloads reductions to the switches, and racks communicate over a RoCE fabric with one 800 Gb/s
ConnectX-8 SuperNIC per GPU. The cluster has 2,016 GPUs in 28 racks, and the main pretraining run used
1,024 of them, while the rest ran other training experiments concurrently (Sec. K.2.1).

Training uses synchronous data parallelism with DeepSpeed ZeRO-2: every GPU holds the full weights, gradients
and optimizer state are partitioned across ranks, and BF16 gradient synchronization overlaps with computation.
Because the model fits without pipeline parallelism, the GPU memory keeps the parameters, each rank’s
optimizer partition, and the activations of a per-device batch of 16 resident, and the large per-device batch
gives the Tensor Cores more matrix-multiplication work and amortizes kernel-launch overhead. Part of each
step runs in frozen feature encoders rather than the trainable model. Depending on the recipe, the runtime
uses TensorRT for video encoding, fused text-encoder operations, efficient attention kernels that keep the
model’s masking rules, PyTorch compilation, and activation recomputation, and it can encode the next batch’s
features while the current batch synchronizes gradients and updates the optimizer. We judge efficiency across
the whole step, from input retrieval and feature encoding to gradient synchronization and the optimizer update.
A hardware-counter-based utilization metric, Overall FLOP Utilization (Pedersen et al., 2026), is monitored
alongside step time, but elapsed time and model quality decide whether a change is kept, since an optimization
can shorten the step without moving the utilization signal (Sec. K.4).

4.7.2. Training Data Loading
Training data are stored in LanceDS, built on the Lance columnar format (Pace et al., 2025; Lance Contributors,
2026). Each dataset holds a step table with state, actions, annotations, and quality-control (QC) signals, an
episode table, and a media table with the original videos. Lance versioning preserves earlier snapshots when
episodes are added or annotations and QC signals change, and new QC columns are added without rewriting
existing ones. A training run pins one snapshot per dataset, so its data, normalization statistics, and metadata
are reproduced together. All datasets share one schema, and the recipe’s embodiment tag selects at read time
the state and action groups, cameras, annotations, and normalization statistics of that embodiment, as well
as the model’s embodiment-specific projection layers. QC rules are not applied in the loading path: they are
evaluated offline over the temporal window that each training sample reads, its observations and its action
targets, and stored as views of the eligible sample starts. The views are rebuilt with every new snapshot and
carry normalization statistics computed over the samples they keep. Sampling is drawn on demand from
deterministic per-rank seeds and mixes shards so that each global step follows the recipe’s dataset proportions.
Batch preparation runs in three asynchronous stages: video shards are prefetched from shared storage to
node-local NVMe, frames are decoded (about 90% of preparation time in a three-view shard benchmark), and
samples are augmented, collated, and copied to the GPU from pinned memory. Variable-length token packing
reduces padding. Over 100 steady-state pretraining steps on 1,024 GB300 GPUs, reading 25 dataset stores with
15.6 million episode records and 46.2 billion time steps, the loader delivered about 12,000 sample windows
per second, and the mean exposed wait for input was 0.37 ms (0.61 ms at the 99th percentile), or 0.049% of
the measured step time (Tab. 35). These numbers characterize input delivery, not the model’s FLOP utilization.
In the measured run, packing filled 94.5% of the positions in 32,768-token rows. Loader changes are validated
in closed-loop simulation before adoption (Sec. 4.5.2), and Sec. K.4.3 details the loader.

4.7.3. Multi-Modal Data Processing
Before they can be loaded, raw human demonstration recordings are converted into training-ready data.
Multi-camera video is retrieved, decoded, and resized. Motion records are transformed and, where needed,
retargeted to the training hand representation. Observations, actions, and annotations are then aligned in
time and packaged as indexed episodes in training-ready shards. The pipeline processes petabyte-scale input
each month, with individual production jobs using nearly 10,000 vCPUs. The central difficulty is that these
conversions share inputs and compete for limited working storage. A long video can contain many episodes,
and one episode can depend on several camera files and separately stored motion records, so a shared input
can be reclaimed only after its slowest consumer finishes, and a slow conversion or upload can block the
retrieval of unrelated inputs and leave workers idle. The pipeline therefore ties the lifetime of each input to
execution progress. Files stream from object storage directly to the processing nodes, without staging on a
shared filesystem, so conversion starts before a collection has been fully retrieved. New inputs are admitted

                   GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



against each node’s storage budget, counting resident files, pending transfers, and output headroom, and
work is dispatched to the node that holds its inputs. An episode releases its claim on an input only after its
outputs are uploaded, so the completion of consumers, rather than prefetching, governs when new inputs enter.
After an interruption, lost inputs are rebuilt through the same admission path, and durable checkpoints let the
resumed job reuse completed conversions and published shards. In three production jobs on 100 nodes with 96

training-ready shard within 28 minutes, and averaged 97.8% CPU utilization during conversion. Across five
interrupted jobs, 92.4% of the completed conversion time went to work that did not have to be repeated, and
publication resumed 2.4 to 5.3 minutes after a restart (Sec. K.6 and Tab. 36).

5. Conclusion and Open Problems
GR00T 2 is a world-action model: a single 2B-parameter transformer that predicts future observations and
robot actions together, pretrained on egocentric human video, handheld UMI demonstrations, and teleoperation
of gripper arms, dexterous hands, and a humanoid. Predicting the future gives all of these sources a common
objective, including human video that carries no robot actions. On 50 dexterous tasks new to the robot, the
resulting model succeeds zero-shot in 40% of trials and reaches 95% with five demonstrations (Sec. 3.1). It
learns robot tasks without teleoperated demonstrations of those tasks (Sec. 3.2). Real-world RL raises its
success on contact-rich skills to 88 to 98% (Sec. 3.3). With corrective data and a frontier agent, it completes
cluttered long-horizon assemblies in at least 97% of trials (Sec. 3.4). GR00T 2 Turbo runs onboard a humanoid
and directs a learned whole-body controller (Sec. 3.5).
Three observations from building GR00T 2 shape the problems that follow.
   • Much of what the robot learns need not come from robot data. Robot success on new tasks more than
     doubles with human video in pretraining and does not level off at the largest scale we tested (Fig. 10). For
     glove and Gripper UMI demonstrations, task-agnostic data from the target robot specify the embodiment,
     while the demonstrations specify the task (Sec. 3.2).
   • Reliability comes from complementary mechanisms, not from pretraining alone. Pretraining provides
     breadth. Corrective demonstrations and RL close gaps in precision and contact. The agent supplies
     memory, verification, and recovery over long horizons.
   • Progress depended on measurement. Blinded head-to-head evaluation and calibrated simulation (Sec. 4.3
     and 4.5.2) turned close comparisons into decisions.
Each observation also points to problems that remain open. We group them by part of the robot learning
system, and start each group from the basic constraint that makes it hard.
Model. A robot policy learns from data dominated by observation rather than action, and it must act at the
rate of the physical world. Joint video-action prediction turns every video into supervision, but most of the
tokens it predicts encode future video, much of which does not bear on the next action. GR00T 2 Turbo predicts
a spatial latent grid four times smaller than that of GR00T 2. After post-training, it loses 6.7 points of success
on YAM but half of its success on dexterous tasks, falling from 68.6% to 34.3% (Tab. 1). The comparison does
not isolate the cause, but it suggests that the visual detail a policy must predict depends on the task and the
embodiment. Four questions follow.
   • What to predict. It is unknown which aspects of the future a policy should predict, and at what resolution
     and horizon. It is also unclear whether prediction helps mainly as a training signal or also as imagination
     at inference time.
   • Memory. GR00T 2 observes 0.8 s of history, and tasks that require memory are delegated to the agent
     (Sec. 3.4). In imitation learning, longer raw context can invite shortcuts such as copying past ac-
     tions (de Haan et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2020), which suggests that memory should be structured rather
     than simply longer.
   • Force and touch. GR00T 2 handles force only as an output: from DexUMI demonstrations, it predicts


                                                                                                               26
                     GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



     joint torques that set the hand’s compliance (Sec. 4.4.3). It does not take touch as input, and tactile
     recordings are scarce compared with video. How to add force and touch as inputs without eroding visual
     priors, and at which control rate, is open.
   • Interfaces in the control hierarchy. Image-space points tell GR00T 2 where to act but not how, so
     requirements such as pouring or scooping still rest on language and learned skills (Sec. 3.4). Below
     GR00T 2 Turbo, the interface to SONIC is a motion latent decoded into joint position targets (Sec. 3.5),
     so contact force is not represented explicitly. Which representation should pass between reasoning, policy,
     and controller, and how it transfers to new embodiments, is unsettled.
Data. Every hour of robot data requires a robot and an operator, whereas human experience is abundant but
recorded in a different body. GR00T 2 pretrains on about 400,000 hours of human video, 10,000 hours of
UMI demonstrations, and 23,500 hours of teleoperation (Sec. 4.2). Human video brings coverage, UMI brings
task-focused demonstrations in a robot-compatible format, and teleoperation grounds the embodiment.
What is missing is an exchange rate between these sources. It is not known how much robot performance an
hour of each source yields, or how that yield changes with the data already present. It is also not known how
little target-robot data suffices to specify a new embodiment. Simulation raises the same question. Compute
widens coverage, but agreement with the real world still requires measurement and robot time. The scaling
with human video shows only that one of these rates is positive (Fig. 10). Until they are measured, collection
budgets cannot be allocated on principle.
A second question is what makes data useful beyond its quantity. Demonstrations show mostly successful
behavior, whereas RL, world models, and verifiers also need failures and recoveries; the world model we use
for RL is trained on robot episodes that include both (Sec. 4.5.3). Labels of how well each demonstration was
executed could serve as a further training signal.
Evaluation. A success rate measured on real robots is a binomial estimate, and its uncertainty shrinks only
with the square root of the number of trials. Our post-training benchmarks use up to 20 trials per task (Tab. 1).
That is enough to resolve the large gaps between GR00T 2 and its baselines, but not the differences that matter
in deployment. Distinguishing 95% from 99% success takes about 285 trials per policy, and 99% from 99.9%
more than 1,000.1 Absolute rates also drift with station, operator, and day, which is why we rank checkpoints
by blinded head-to-head comparisons (Sec. 4.3). Even then, the independent units are tasks rather than trials,
and 30 to 35% of comparisons are ties (Sec. D.7). Three directions follow.
   • Proxy evaluation at deployment scale. Across 25 YAM tasks, simulation predicts the real-robot task progress
     of 14 generalist checkpoints with a Pearson correlation of 0.93 (Sec. 4.5.2). It remains to be shown
     whether simulation is as predictive for contact-rich assembly, for RL-trained policies that exploit simulator
     errors, and for comparisons between training recipes rather than checkpoints.
   • Automated verification. In our long-horizon assemblies, three of the eight failures were screws left partly
     driven that both GR00T 2 and the agent judged complete (Sec. G.1). Verifying subtle physical state is a
     prerequisite for autonomous evaluation, for RL rewards, and for deciding when to ask a person for help.
   • Cost to reliability. Robot learning rarely reports the quantity that determines deployment: the robot time
     and human time needed to bring a new task to a target reliability. The same accounting, under matched
     budgets, would settle when RL is worth more than additional demonstrations or corrections.
Hardware. A model can learn only what the hardware allows people to demonstrate, sensors to record, and
robots to repeat. Our teleoperation cannot demonstrate fast, targeted motions such as the conveyor toss, which
GR00T 2 learned from human demonstrations instead (Sec. 3.2). Capture devices that match the robot shrink
the gap the model must bridge. DexUMI replicates the joints and contact surfaces of the robot hand, so on
that hand its demonstrations need no robot play data (Sec. 4.4). This argues for designing robots and capture
devices together, with shared kinematics, camera placement, and tactile formats.

B.1.1. Human Data Capture and Conversion
Human data capture. Each demonstration is recorded with a lightweight egocentric rig: a head-mounted
camera provides the ego view, wrist-mounted cameras provide close-up views of hand-object contact, and the
hands are tracked by a motion-capture glove (Manus) paired with a 6-DoF tracker (PICO) rigidly attached
to each wrist. The glove provides per-finger joint angles, while the tracker provides the global wrist pose.
Together they yield a full 3D hand pose at every timestep without any robot in the loop. Because the tracker’s
IMU does not coincide with the anatomical wrist, we calibrate the tracker-to-wrist rigid transform per session:
an annotator clicks fingertip locations in a handful of frames spanning diverse hand poses, and we solve
for the transform that minimizes the reprojection error of the glove-predicted fingertips. Camera intrinsics
and extrinsics are verified in the same tool by overlaying the tracked controller pose onto the video, so that
calibration errors are caught before any data is converted. The raw stream (ego and wrist video, wrist poses,
finger joints, and language annotations) is captured at 60 Hz, stored in a time-synchronized format and
quality-checked by visualizing the reconstructed hand trajectory against the video.
From human data to robot data. The captured human trajectories are converted into robot-space supervision
in a single offline pass, with no task-specific robot teleoperation. First, each wrist pose is anchored globally
in the gravity-aligned robot base frame: the human’s head camera is placed at the robot’s head camera,
and a per-task offset shifts the hands into the robot workspace. The converted trajectories therefore carry
absolute wrist poses in the robot frame, not wrist displacements alone. Second, the human finger configuration
is retargeted to the robot hand via inverse kinematics on fingertip targets, producing joint commands that
respect the robot’s kinematic limits while preserving the task-relevant contact geometry rather than the literal
human pose. Third, the ego and wrist camera streams are kept as the policy’s visual inputs, so that the
resulting episodes, downsampled to 30 Hz, have the same observation and action schema as native robot data.
During post-training, we sample converted human episodes and generic, non-task-specific robot play data with
equal probability. This co-training grounds the model in the robot’s appearance and dynamics, while human
demonstrations provide all task-specific supervision.

B.1.2. Geometric Retargeting
The role of our retargeting solver is to bring sub-millimeter accuracy to robot finger tips with kHz computation
speed. Given that the conversion is solely offline without human supervision, we ask for automated singularity
handling near workspace boundary and robust redundancy DoF management. To achieve this we make use of
a closed-form geometric inverse kinematic solving technique introduced in Chen et al. (2026b) and Kong et al.
(2026) to handle both the 7-DoF arms and 22 DoF robot hands. Given 6D pose data of ego camera and left /
right wrist mount, we first find the ideal placement of human data in robot frame for each task. The solver
then retargets the hands before the arm, and both stages track fingertip targets. A unified “palm-box” hand
frame introduced in Kong et al. (2026), anchored at the index fingertip, aligns human and robot hands. The
22-DoF hand solver places the robot thumb tip relative to the solved index fingertip, which preserves the pinch
geometry, while the other fingers follow the human bone directions. The arm’s geometric inverse-kinematics
solver then tracks the palm-box origin, so the robot index fingertip lands on the human index fingertip.
Our combined arm-hand solver achieves approximately 118,000 bimanual solves per second on a single core of
an Intel Core Ultra 9 285K CPU, with a mean native computation time of 8.48 𝜇s, excluding Python interface
overhead. Across 1,024 synthetic in-range wrist targets, maximum forward-kinematics-verified pose errors
remain below 10−15 m and 10−15 rad. This throughput supports real-time teleoperation (Sec. C.3.1) and offline
human-data conversion.

B.1.3. Co-Training with Robot Play Data
GR00T 2 turns retargeted human demonstrations into a robot policy by co-training them with robot play data.
Starting from its cross-embodiment pretrained checkpoint, GR00T 2 fine-tunes on two complementary sources:
retargeted human demonstrations of the target task, which contain the task but not the robot, and task-agnostic
play data from the target robot, collected with the teleoperation system of Sec. C.3.1, which contain the robot
but not the task. Human data teach GR00T 2 what to do: the task intent, the object interactions, and the
motion strategy, such as which hand leads, which fingers make contact, and how fast the wrist moves. Play
data teach GR00T 2 how this robot looks and moves: its appearance, kinematics, and dynamics, which stay the
same across tasks, so one play pool serves every task. Without teleoperation, the play data are the only robot
data in post-training. Each new task therefore adds only cheap human demonstrations.
Human and robot episodes share one data format, so the two sources mix freely within a training batch. Both
provide three camera views (ego and two wrists) at 30 Hz, a 62-D state of absolute wrist poses in the robot
base frame and hand-joint positions, and a 62-D action chunk of wrist poses relative to the current state and
absolute hand-joint positions.

B.1.4. Learning New Tasks without Teleoperation
GR00T 2 learns new robot tasks from human demonstrations alone. Four tasks on the Tianji, a bimanual robot
with two 7-DoF arms and two 22-DoF Sharpa hands, test this claim and stress two parts of the embodiment
gap. Three chemistry-lab tasks handle small, often transparent labware and test precision under the kinematic
gap, where retargeting error and the larger robot hand both consume the tolerance. The dynamic task tests the
kinodynamic gap on a fast, targeted throw that teleoperation cannot demonstrate (Fig. 26).

C.1. Human Data for GR00T 2
Prior work in scaling data for robot learning has showcased emergent transfer and generalization through

C.1.1. Dataset Composition
The Human-1M inventory used within GR00T 2 contains 402,730 hours across approximately 13.75 million
episodes, spanning over 51,500 unique collectors and 123,000 scenes.
Collection Settings. We collect demonstrations in both natural “in-the-wild” and controlled studio environ-
ments. In-the-wild collection captures proficient laborers in economically valuable scenarios across households,
industry workplaces, agriculture, commercial kitchens, service, retail, and logistics. Studio collection con-
centrates on tabletop manipulation under standardized protocols, resembling demonstration style robotics
datasets. Studio data comprises of assembly tasks that involve assembling various kits, curated tasks selected
to cover target robot skills and task distributions. Within studio data, “in-lab” collection follows a stricter
protocol where demonstrators keep both hands in view, avoid idle periods, and perform motions at speeds
chosen to match the robot. Approximately 80,000 hours of the total mixture is studio data. These settings serve
complementary purposes. Natural environments broaden the range of interactions observed during pretraining
for the world-action model, while controlled collection makes it possible to target particular skills and capture
them under more consistent conditions for better transfer to the downstream robot embodiments.
Egocentric Cameras. The dataset includes monocular and stereo egocentric recordings. Monocular setups use
a single head-mounted RGB camera. Stereo setups provide synchronized two camera RGB head views with
optional additional multi-view cameras for better hand pose estimation.
Wrist Cameras. Approximately 90,000 hours of the collection also includes wrist cameras, which provide
close-up views of manipulation and hand-object interactions to reduce partial observability during fine-grained
manipulation. Additionally, a portion of the studio data contains robot-aligned wrist viewpoints to address the
visual alignment gap.
Even when humans and robots perform the same grasp, wrist-camera placement can change the apparent size
and orientation of the hand and object, as well as the visibility of their interaction. We studied this alignment
process with the Sharpa platform. We screened 13 initial mount designs and conducted a controlled comparison
of four mounts across four operators. The human and robot perform the same four thumb-finger grasps and
their viewpoints are compared in a 640x480 image frame. The evaluation measured hand-object overlap, object
scale, camera-to-object distance, and camera orientation. We further optimized image rotation and scaling
for visual alignment, and used ChArUco (Garrido-Jurado et al., 2014) calibration together with directional
motions to optimize angular alignment.
Annotations. Each egocentric episode contains language annotations along with frame-aligned estimations
of hand and head motion following prior work on learning from egocentric human experience (Kareer et al.,
2025; Punamiya et al., 2026).


                                                                                                              57
                    GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



Hand and Camera Pose. Hand motion is represented by 21 three-dimensional keypoints per hand: the wrist
and four landmarks along each finger, including the fingertips. Keypoint positions are expressed in meters in
the reference head-camera coordinate frame and aligned with the corresponding video frames. Head motion
is represented by the six-degree-of-freedom pose of the head-mounted reference camera, estimated using
simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) in a canonical per episode world frame.
Language Annotation. Language describes demonstrations at two levels. Tier 1 (T1) annotations provide
a one- to two-sentence episode summary identifying the primary goal, major actions, and key objects. Tier
2 (T2) annotations describe individual actions in contiguous, timestamped segments of 1-10 seconds. Each
description is self-contained and specifies the relevant hand, action, and objects. These annotations support
language-conditioned learning and task-based data curation. The language annotations are validated by human
quality control checks. The highest quality annotations are used for the training mixture of Flora pipeline
described in Sec. C.4. The Flora pipeline is then used to further enrich or provide T2 language annotations
across the dataset.

C.1.2. Data Filtering and Quality Control
The usefulness of a human demonstration for tabletop manipulation settings depends on both the interaction it
contains and the reliability of its recorded motion. We therefore apply quality control at two levels. Batch-level
review checks the content and annotations of a delivery, while quantitative filtering selects timesteps with
sufficiently stable observations and hand trajectories. Additional details about the quality control are in the
accompanying Human-1M dataset release.
Human review. Human auditors check language-annotation validity, task quality and idle time as per our
collection specs at a 95% per episode pass rate. These criteria screen deliveries before individual training
timesteps are selected by the quantitative filters.
Quantitative Filtering. At each candidate training timestep, we evaluate a window extending 24 frames into
the past and 48 frames into the future. We retain the timestep only if all quality criteria pass over this window.
The checks constrain head rotation and translation, hand visibility, consistency of reconstructed bone lengths,
and wrist speed (Tab. 8). The bone-length error of a hand in a frame is the median absolute deviation of its 20
finger-bone lengths from their per-episode medians, divided by the mean of those medians, so the threshold of
0.05 corresponds to 5% of the mean bone length.

C.1.3. In-Lab Human Data
In-lab data is the part of human data that transfers most directly to a robot policy. Two gaps separate a human
demonstration from a robot rollout, and both can be narrowed at collection time rather than left to the model.
The observation gap: in-the-wild wrist cameras are not placed with reference to any robot, so the hand appears
at a different angle, scale, and position than in the robot’s wrist view, and the model must imagine across this
viewpoint change before it can imagine across embodiments (Sec. B.1.5). The motion gap: natural human
execution is faster than teleoperated robot execution, interleaves pauses and task-irrelevant gestures, and uses
motions the robot hand cannot reproduce. In-lab collection follows our studio collection guidelines, which fix
camera placement, motion, and task protocol with the target robot in mind.


                                                                                                               58
                      GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



Collection protocol. Demonstrators keep the head still, keep both hands and the manipulated objects visible
in every frame, avoid idle time, rehearse each task before recording, and follow a reference video where one
exists. Pace is controlled through bounds on wrist speed, which exclude motion too fast for the robot on one
side and near-stationary hands on the other. Each task is recorded by several operators in several environments,
and episodes that a robot could not reproduce are rejected before delivery. The complete criteria, including the
acceptance thresholds for the wrist camera and for hand-pose quality, are listed in Sec. C.1.4.
Alignment to the target embodiment. The target is Tianji with Sharpa hands, the platform of the human-to-
robot transfer (Sec. B.1), whose teleoperation system is described in Sec. C.3.1. On the observation side, the
human wrist camera is mounted to reproduce the view of the robot’s wrist camera. It is accepted only when
the hand and the object appear at the same scale and position as in the robot view. The head camera stands in
for the robot’s head camera and is held still, and demonstrators whose hands are close to the robot hand in size
are preferred. On the motion side, tasks mirror the robot’s teleoperation tasks with the same objects and step
order, robot-paired tasks come with reference videos recorded on the robot, and the speed bounds keep the
pace within what the robot can execute. In-lab episodes pass through the same filtering and annotation as the
rest of Human-1M (Tab. 8, Sec. C.4), and their hand keypoints can be retargeted with the solver of Sec. B.1.2
into the observation and action schema of a teleoperated Tianji episode (Sec. B.1.3).
Scale and diversity. In-lab collection has delivered about 3,000 hours and 700k episodes. The tasks are drawn
from more than 2,000 task specifications, which span short single-goal tabletop tasks that each isolate a grasp
or a skill, robot-paired tasks with fixed objects and reference videos that stress dexterity and dynamics, longer
multi-step chores, and tasks paired with UMI reference demonstrations (Sec. C.2), so that the same task exists
as human, UMI, and robot data. Episodes are short and dense, and each task is demonstrated hundreds of
times by different operators, which gives the model many views of the same manipulation rather than many
unrelated clips.

C.1.5. Scaling Human Video
GR00T 2 pretrains on egocentric human video from Human-1M as well as on robot data. To measure how
much the human video contributes to robot performance, we train four models that differ in how much human
video they see. We test each one on new tasks on Dexter, a bimanual robot with two 7-DoF arms and two
22-DoF Sharpa hands, and on the video rollouts each model generates for the same tasks.
Setup. The four models share the GR00T 2 architecture and train on the same Dexter teleoperation data with
the same recipe. They differ only in the human video they see. The 0% model sees none. The other three
start from a common human-video pretraining checkpoint, then continue on 1%, 10%, or 100% of the tasks in
the multi-view human video of Human-1M (0.87k, 8.1k, and 84.4k hours after the timestep filters of Tab. 8)
and co-train on it with the Dexter data. The 0% model does not have this shared stage, so comparisons with
it measure the shared stage and the human share together, whereas the 1%, 10%, and 100% models differ
only in the share and the matching training length. Training length is matched in epochs. Each human share
is pretrained for about 0.5 epoch of that share, so the larger shares also take more steps, and every model,
including the 0% model, is then trained for 3 epochs of the Dexter data, where one epoch passes once over
every step of every 30 Hz trajectory. During this stage, the 1%, 10%, and 100% models draw 45% of their
training samples from Dexter and 55% from human video, while the 0% model trains on the Dexter data alone.
Multi-view human video is recorded with wrist cameras as well as a head camera, like the robot. GR00T 2
itself pretrains on 403k hours of Human-1M. We pick each model’s checkpoint offline with the same rule for all
four, without robot evaluation.
We test on 72 Dexter tasks, giving each model only the task instruction. 22 of them are out-of-distribution (OOD)
tasks. They use objects and pick-and-place skills from Dexter’s training data, with new kinds of instructions
such as color, negation, size, order, count, and spatial relations. The other 50 never appear in Dexter’s training
data, and 47 of them are in Dexter-50. Each model runs 3 episodes per task, for 864 episodes in total. On each
task, the four models run back to back on the same station and scene, in the order 0%, 1%, 10%, 100%. One
operator grades all of them on whether the task’s end state is reached. We also score task progress, the fraction
of the task completed, with a vision-language model on the real robot rollout video from the head and both
wrist cameras.
GR00T 2 also predicts video, so we evaluate the same 72 tasks without a robot as well. From 3 recorded start
scenes per task, each model generates video rollouts for the task instruction with 10 generation seeds, for 2,160
videos per model. The same vision-language model judges whether a rollout performs the commanded action
on the target object (video success) and scores its task progress with the same prompt as the real episodes.
Results. All four metrics rise with the amount of human video (Fig. 10). On the robot, success goes from 18%
without human video to 27%, 28%, and 41% with 1%, 10%, and 100%, and task progress from 50% to 58%,
61%, and 70%. In video rollouts, success goes from 30% to 33%, 38%, and 51%, and task progress from 64%
to 66%, 72%, and 77%. Using all of the multi-view human video instead of none raises success on the robot by
23.1 points (95% confidence interval 13.4 to 33.3, paired over tasks) and task progress by 20.1 points (12.3 to
27.9). In video rollouts it raises success by 21.3 points (14.3 to 28.2) and task progress by 12.4 points (7.8 to
17.1).
From 1% to 100%, task progress on the robot gains about 6 points for each tenfold increase in human video
(2.5 to 9.6), and video success about 9 points (5.9 to 12.0). The step from 10% to 100% is clear on every
metric. On the robot, success gains 12.5 points (4.2 to 21.3). The step from 1% to 10% is within noise on the
robot (success +0.9 and progress +3.2 points), but shows in video rollouts (success +4.7 points, 0.1 to 9.4,
and progress +6.2 points, 2.6 to 9.9), which average 30 rollouts per task instead of 3. None of the metrics
levels off at 100%, which suggests that more human video would help further. Each data share is a single
training run, so the error bars show how much the results vary across repeated rollouts, not across training
runs.

C.2. UMI-10K for GR00T 2
Scale. The Gripper UMI corpus (App. E) contributes 942k quality-controlled episodes and 10,184 hours of
bimanual parallel-gripper demonstrations across 2,897 tasks to GR00T 2 pretraining (Tab. 10). The corpus is
built for breadth, focusing on short to mid-horizon tasks with wide behavior coverage, rather than depth on a
few specialized tasks: no task accounts for more than 1.2% of the hours, and the instructions span 356 object
categories and 49 skills in our manipulation vocabulary.
Task distribution. We characterize the corpus by tagging every task instruction with a 49-skill vocabulary
organized in four groups by manipulation and object type: prehensile, non-prehensile, articulated, and
deformable, the same vocabulary that drives task generation (below). The skill distribution is shown in

skill to ensure diversity and complexity of actions captured. 29% of episodes involve a dynamic skill such
as throwing, toppling, shaking, or sweeping, and 17% involve a precision skill such as insertion or capping.
Articulated-object skills, drawers, lids, zippers, box flaps, doors, knobs, and cranks, appear in 29% of episodes,
and deformable-object skills, folding, spreading, rolling, tearing, and tying, in 15%. Tasks are graded into
three difficulty tiers along two axes, the number of sequential subgoals and the share of more complex motion
beyond pick-and-place, which both increase with difficulty tier. By hours, 41% of the corpus is simple (two or
more subgoals, any motion), 38% moderate (three or more state-dependent subgoals with at least one non-pick-
and-place skill), and 21% complex (four or more state-dependent subgoals dominated by non-pick-and-place
skills).

corrections feed back into the judge. Together these stages filter out roughly a quarter of recorded episodes

C.4. Data Annotations
We built Flora (Leng et al., 2026), the data engine that annotates training data for GR00T 2 models. Flora
consists of three models: Flora-TempSeg localizes actions in time, Flora-Captioner describes each localized
action, and Flora-Rewarder produces outcome, dense, and rubric-based rewards. They are trained on open-
source, licensed, and in-house data, where human annotators provide dense timestamped captions and
action-quality labels under detailed labeling guidelines. The same models label human egocentric video and
robot recordings from parallel-gripper and dexterous-hand embodiments, with one or more synchronized
camera views.


                                                                                                                                                   63
                   GR00T 2: Scaling Robot Foundation Models for Generalization, Dexterity, and Adaptation



Timestamped captions. Given an episode video, Flora outputs a set of time intervals, each paired with a
caption of the action performed in it. Flora-TempSeg runs on overlapping windows and predicts the start
and end time of each action in a window, together with flags that mark actions continuing past the window
edges. These predictions are mapped to episode time and stitched into episode-level intervals, while concurrent
actions, such as two hands doing different things, remain separate intervals. Flora-Captioner then captions
each interval independently. In addition to the frames of the interval, it sees frames from the neighboring
actions before and after it, which help resolve object identity and intent, while the caption describes only
the target interval. Synchronized camera views are tiled into one image per time step. Each caption is a
self-contained imperative instruction that names the intended action, the objects, the role of each hand or arm,
and relevant spatial relations, for example “hold tea leaves with right hand, pass tea leaves to left hand”. Failed
attempts are captioned with their intended goal, without implying success. Both models are first fine-tuned on
human-annotated captions and then trained with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) on verifiable rewards: temporal F1
and boundary accuracy for Flora-TempSeg, and reference-grounded coverage of caption requirements with a
penalty for factual errors for Flora-Captioner.
Quality and cost. On WGO-Bench (Macrodata Labs, 2026), Flora achieves the best temporal segmentation
F1 of all evaluated models, 50.3 versus 46.0 for the next-best model, GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026). On our
300-episode in-house captioning benchmark, it leads all models on human episodes and ranks second to Astra
on robot episodes.
Annotating GR00T 2 data. For GR00T 2, Flora turns raw episodes into training-ready supervision. These
instructions provide fine-grained language conditioning for pretraining. At the production rate above, relabeling
the roughly 400k hours of Human-1M data used by GR00T 2 takes about two days.

K.6. Scalable Multi-Modal Data Processing
Scaling GR00T 2 across tasks and environments requires turning increasingly large and diverse collections of
human demonstrations into training-ready data. Our infrastructure processes petabyte-scale monthly input
volumes, with individual production jobs using nearly 10,000 vCPUs. At this scale, preparation must sustain both
massive data movement and heterogeneous computation: multi-camera video must be retrieved, decoded, and
resized. Motion records must be transformed and, where needed, retargeted to the training hand representation,
and observations, actions, and annotations must be aligned in time. Recording duration, resolution, encoding,
and retargeting requirements vary across sources, so equal numbers of episodes can impose very different
I/O and compute demands. The output is a consistent set of indexed multimodal episodes, packaged into
training-ready shards (Fig. 69).
The central challenge is that these conversions share data and compete for limited working storage. A long
video can contain many episodes, while one episode can depend on several camera files and separately stored
motion records. Staging a shared file once avoids repeated downloads, but its slowest consumer determines
when that storage can be reclaimed. A slow conversion or output upload can therefore retain local files, block
retrieval of unrelated inputs, and leave compute workers idle. Interruptions compound the problem by adding
repeated transfers and expensive recomputation. Sustained throughput requires coordinating data lifetime
with execution progress across the entire pipeline.
The ingestion pipeline coordinates execution with the lifetime of its shared inputs: new work is admitted
according to committed storage, dispatched where its inputs reside, and releases capacity only after its outputs
are safely uploaded. Active files move directly from object storage to processing nodes, allowing conversion to
begin before the entire selected collection has been retrieved. This removes intermediate staging on a shared
filesystem while making input placement, retention, and reconstruction explicit responsibilities. Fig. 70 shows

Admit and place work (Fig. 70, panels B and C). Equal episode counts can imply very different file footprints
and sharing patterns, so task limits alone cannot control storage demand. Before downloading an input group,
admission accounts for resident files, pending transfers, and output headroom against the node’s budget.
Required inputs must be ready before episode dispatch, and consumers execute on the node holding those
inputs. Separate Ray worker pools (Moritz et al., 2018) give loading, conversion, and publication independent
concurrency. Bounded queues limit outstanding work while storage admission limits the capacity committed to
it.
Retain inputs through completion, then release capacity (Fig. 70, panels A to C). A downloaded video
may serve several episodes, including consumers still waiting for execution. The loader establishes their claims
before handing off work, protecting shared inputs across queueing, conversion, and upload. An episode releases
its claims only after its intermediate results have been uploaded and temporary outputs cleaned up, while other
consumers continue to protect the same files. Once the last claim is released, cleanup can reclaim those files and
make room for the next input group. Consumer completion thus governs admission of subsequent inputs.
Retrieval, conversion, and shard publication advance asynchronously around this dependency: they overlap
across workers without allowing prefetch to grow independently of the storage that unfinished consumers still
hold.
Recover within the same capacity budget (Fig. 70, panel D). Recovery preserves this connection between
execution and storage. Stopped attempts release obsolete claims, while lost inputs are reconstructed on an
available node through the same admission path before affected episodes retry. Durable conversion-group and
publication checkpoints separately identify completed results that job resumption can reuse. Input release
after upload and cleanup, and checkpoint-based reuse after restart, are distinct boundaries: an uploaded
artifact without recorded completion may still require repeated processing. These rules keep retries within the
working-storage budget while preserving reusable progress.
This coordination of shared-input lifetime, admission, and recovery complements prior work on video analysis,
multimodal processing, and prefetching (Poms et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2025; Luan et al., 2026; Dryden et al.,
2021).
